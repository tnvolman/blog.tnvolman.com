Not a Chatbot With a Bible Verse

Referencing Scripture is not the same as submitting to it. One is a costume. The other is a constraint. I built the constraint.

Michael McClanahan · August 30, 2026 · 6 min read

I didn't go looking for a product gap.

On April 25, God asked me a question. Not audibly — but with the kind of clarity that doesn't leave room for debate. How could agentic AI advance the Kingdom? I sat with that question for about forty-eight hours before I started moving. And when I moved, the first thing I did was look at what already existed.

What I found bothered me more than nothing would have.

There were tools. Polished ones. Tools that could drop John 3:16 into a sentence with confidence and then quietly invent doctrine in the next one. YouVersion's CEO said the best models still misquote Scripture at least 15 percent of the time. Some as high as 60. Verses invented. Verses twisted. Verses just slightly off in a way most people wouldn't catch — and that's the dangerous kind. Not wrong enough to flag. Just wrong enough to mislead someone who trusted it at the worst moment of their life.

That's not a rounding error. That's a theological liability dressed up in a clean UI.

Here's the technical problem underneath the spiritual one.

Large language models — the engines running most AI tools — are trained on massive datasets pulled from the internet. They are, by design, pattern-matching machines. They predict the next most likely word based on everything they've been fed. They are extraordinarily good at sounding right. That's not a bug. That's the architecture.

But sounding right and being right are two entirely different things when you're handling the Word of God.

When a model is asked a theological question, it doesn't search a Bible. It searches its weights — billions of numerical parameters shaped by everything it was trained on. Reddit threads. Theology blogs. Sermon transcripts. Commentary sections. Heterodox forums. All of it weighted and compressed into something that produces fluent, confident, plausible-sounding output.

The model has no conviction. It has no submission. It has pattern recognition at scale.

And here's the part that stopped me cold: you can't tell the difference by reading the output. That's what makes it dangerous for the Church. A pastor in a small congregation — no seminary staff, no theologian down the hall — asks an AI tool a hard question about suffering or divorce or baptism. The answer comes back warm, articulate, and wrong. And he doesn't know.

That's not a product problem. That's a shepherding problem.

So I built a gate.

Not a filter. Not a content policy. A gate.

Inside what I'm building — a pastoral decision-support tool called Sanctuary — there is an agent whose only job is theological review. His name is Arch. He doesn't generate content. He doesn't answer questions. He reads what the system produces and asks exactly one question:

Is this true to the Word of God?

That's it. That's the whole job description.

Arch doesn't cross-reference the internet. He doesn't consult theological trends or denominational preferences. He works from Scripture — the original Hebrew, Greek, and Aramaic, with the King James as the working English — as the final and binding authority. If the output passes, it moves forward. If it doesn't, it doesn't. No exceptions. No workarounds. The gate is the gate.

In software architecture, we call this a hard constraint — a rule the system cannot route around, override, or negotiate with. Most AI systems are built with soft constraints: guidelines, system prompts, safety rails that can be talked around with the right framing. A hard constraint is different. It's structural. It's load-bearing. You don't get past it by being clever.

That's what Arch is. Not a feature. Not a selling point. A structural commitment to the authority of Scripture baked into the architecture itself.

If you're not being filled with the Spirit, you're being filled by something.

That's not just a spiritual principle. It's a systems principle.

Every model is filled with something. The question is what. Most of the Christian AI tools I evaluated were filled with the internet — and then decorated with verses. That decoration is not submission. It's costuming. And the difference matters enormously when the person on the other end is a pastor trying to counsel a grieving widow or a church leader trying to navigate a split congregation.

A model trained on everything and constrained by nothing isn't spiritually neutral. Neutrality doesn't exist in a system that's always generating output. It's filled. With whatever went in. And whatever went in comes out — fluently, confidently, and at scale.

Sanctuary is built on a different filling principle. Not verses taped on top. The Word as the last word. Every time. By design. In the architecture.

That's not a marketing claim. That's a hard constraint enforced by an agent who has exactly one job.

I build this in stolen hours.

Full-time in pharmaceutical supply chain by day. Evenings and weekends, I'm here — writing code, wiring agents, reading theology, thinking about the pastor in a congregation of 80 who has never had a theologian on staff, a seminary professor on speed dial, or a budget for anything beyond keeping the lights on. The one who loves his people fiercely and is doing his best with what he has.

That pastor is why Sanctuary exists.

Not because the market asked for it. Not because I saw a business opportunity in the faith space. Because God asked me a question on April 25 and I said yes. And saying yes meant building something that actually submits to the Word — not something that references it. Not something that sounds right. Something that is constrained by it. Structurally. Architecturally. All the way down.

A lot of Christian AI can quote a verse.

I built the gate.
